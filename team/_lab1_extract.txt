​4. Demo / Viva : (5 Marks)​
​The​​team presents and demos togethe​r in one shared​​session — but within it, each​​member is scored​
 ​individually​​on their own contribution. Showing up​​prepared for your own portion is on you; it isn't​
  ​averaged across the team.​
​Format​

    ​●​ ​The team walks through the demo together, but each member presents and defends their own​
         ​models across all 3 tasks.​

​What to bring​

    ​●​ ​Your own trained models/checkpoints, ready to run or demo live if asked.​
      ​●​ ​Your own results.md and metrics tables for all 3 tasks, open and ready to reference.​

​What you'll be asked to explain(any or all the below)​

      ​●​      ​Your architecture and hyperparameter choices for each task, and why you made them.​
       ​●​      ​How your reported metrics were computed.​
        ​●​      ​Your failure/error analysis findings (Task 1 failure cases, Task 2 error review).​
         ​●​      ​How your model differs from your teammates' models and what that comparison showed.​

​Graded on (per individual, not per team)​

      ​●​ ​Depth of understanding of your own implementation — not just what it does, but why.​
       ​●​ ​Ability to justify design decisions and answer follow-up or "what if" questions.​
        ​●​ ​Clarity of explanation.​

​Your leaderboard rank or benchmark score is not what's being judged here — your understanding of​
 ​your own work is. A confident teammate cannot carry you: if you can't explain your own model, that​
  ​reflects in your own score only.​




​5. Reproducibility & Professional Practice (5 marks — Team)​
​This is graded once for the whole team, based on the shared repo. Two things are expected of every​
 ​member individually, but they support the team score rather than being marked separately:​
      ​●​ ​Keep an unedited raw log file for every training run — this is your evidence trail; do not clean it​
           ​up after the fact.​
      ​●​ ​Record a manifest for your own work: package/library versions, environment file, and which​
           ​checkpoint maps to which result in your report.​

​Graded criteria (5 marks)​
      ​●​ ​The README must let a grader clone the repo and reproduce a smoke test (any one member's​
           ​run) using a single documented command.​
      ​●​ ​No hard-coded personal file paths or secrets anywhere in the repo; config-driven runs only.​
      ​●​ ​Every member's raw logs and manifests are present and untouched since the run.​

​8. Best Practice Tips​
   ​●​ ​Commit early and often, with clear messages — your commit history is part of the evidence the​
        ​team is graded on.​
   ​●​ ​Agree as a team on one evaluation script per task before anyone starts training, so every​
        ​member's metrics are directly comparable.​
   ​●​ ​Use a shared config file (YAML/JSON) per model instead of hard-coded values — it makes your​
        ​run reproducible and easy to compare.​
   ​●​ ​Keep a running experiment log (what you tried, what changed, what happened) — it turns into​
        ​your failure/error analysis almost for free.​
   ​●​ ​You​​CAN’T​​use Claude (or a similar AI assistant) -​​for the core architecture decisions and analysis​
        ​must be your own understanding, since you'll be asked to defend them.​
   ​●​ ​Drafts, review and test everything yourself before committing — you are accountable for what's​
        ​pushed to the repo.​
    ​●​ ​Sync with your team regularly, not just at the end — the team score depends on catching​
         ​mismatched protocols or formats early, not fixing them the night before submission.​
    ​●​ ​Back up​​checkpoints and logs outside your local machine​​(repo, cloud storage) —​​lost training​
         ​runs cannot be re-created before the deadline.​


​Task 1 Build a GPT-Style LLM From​
 ​Scratch​
  ​Your Role: AI Research Engineer, Language Modeling Team​
   ​You've been asked to build a from-scratch reference GPT implementation on the TinyStories dataset​
    ​— the same exercise used to validate that engineers understand attention, causal masking, and​
     ​autoregressive generation before touching production-scale models. No prebuilt Transformer or​
      ​attention modules allowed.​
​Resources:​​TinyStories dataset​ ​|​ ​TinyStories paper​ ​|​ ​Attention Is All You Need paper​

​Instructions​

​1.1 Data Preprocessing (5 Marks)​
 ​1.​ ​Load the dataset and tokenize the text at the character level.​
 ​2.​ ​Create fixed-length input–target sequences suitable for autoregressive language modelling.​
 ​3.​ ​Convert characters into integer encodings using your own char_to_idx and idx_to_char dictionaries.​
 ​4.​ ​Split the dataset into training (100K) and validation (10K) sets — each member creates their own​
       ​split.​

​1.2 GPT Model Implementation (15 Marks)​
 ​1.​ ​Implement one or more Transformer blocks from scratch, each consisting of: Multi-Head​
       ​Self-Attention, Layer Normalization, Feed-Forward Network, and Residual connections.​
 ​2.​ ​Use causal masking in self-attention so the model cannot access future tokens during training.​
 ​3.​ ​Create learnable embedding layers for token embeddings and positional embeddings.​
 ​4.​ ​Add a language modelling head that projects the final hidden representations to the vocabulary size​
       ​for next-token prediction.​

​1.3 Training and Text Generation (8 Marks)​
 ​1.​ ​Train the model using cross-entropy loss.​
 ​2.​ ​Implement learning rate warm-up and scheduling.​
 ​3.​ ​Train the model for a minimum of 10 epochs.​
 ​4.​ ​Plot and report training and validation loss curves.​
 ​5.​ ​Generate text samples using greedy decoding or temperature-based sampling.​

​1.4 Sequence Model Failure Analysis (2 Marks)​
 ​1.​ ​Analyse three failure cases observed in your generated text.​
 ​2.​ ​For each case, provide the generated text snippet and identify the type of failure (e.g., repetition,​
       ​broken grammar, loss of coherence, hallucination) along with your observation.​
​Task 1 — Evaluation Metrics (report all)​
    ​●​ ​Training cross-entropy loss​
    ​●​ ​Validation cross-entropy loss​
    ​●​ ​Perplexity​
    ​●​ ​Bits-per-character​
    ​●​ ​Generalization gap (train vs. val loss)​
    ​●​ ​Top-1 next-character accuracy​
    ​●​ ​Distinct-1/2/3 (generation diversity)​
    ​●​ ​Repeated 4-gram rate​
    ​●​ ​Gradient norm / training stability (loss spikes, NaNs)​
    ​●​ ​Parameter count​
    ​●​ ​Training tokens/sec and generation tokens/sec​
    ​●​ ​Peak memory usage and total training time​



​Use of prebuilt Transformer or attention modules is​​not permitted​.​



​Task 2 Yelp Polarity Sentiment​
 ​Classification​
  ​Your Role: Applied NLP Researcher, Text Classification Team​
   ​Your team is running a model bake-off for binary sentiment classification with no pretrained​
    ​embeddings or pretrained language models allowed — the same constraint applied ML teams face in​
     ​low-resource or regulated settings. You will build and defend your own baseline-to-experimental​
      ​model lineup.​

​Resources:​​yelp polarity​ ​|​​NLP Sentiment Classification​

​Instructions​

​2.1 Data Preprocessing (10 Marks)​
 ​1.​ ​Analyse review length distribution, class distribution and class balance.​
 ​2.​ ​Handle missing values or malformed entries, if any.​
 ​3.​ ​Perform text preprocessing, including but not limited to:​
              ​○​ ​lowercasing​
             ​○​ ​removal of punctuation and special characters​
             ​○​ ​stopword removal​
             ​○​ ​stemming or lemmatization (if applicable)​
 ​4.​ ​Tokenise the processed text.​
 ​5.​ ​Create textual embeddings learned from scratch.​
​2.2 Model Training and Evaluation (15 Marks)​
 ​1.​ ​Train 3 of your own models: a Baseline, and two Experimental models that each change architecture​
       ​or hyperparameters — your set of 3 must differ from your own other models and from every other​
        ​member's models.​
 ​2.​ ​Justify the model architecture and embedding choices for each model.​
 ​3.​ ​Evaluate each model using the full metrics list below.​
 ​4.​ ​Manually review 20 of your own model's errors: 5 confident false positives, 5 confident false​
       ​negatives, 5 near-threshold errors, 5 slice-specific failures — assign an error type to each and​
        ​propose one testable fix.​
 ​5.​ ​Record the exact CPU/GPU used for training each model.​

​2.3 Comparative Analysis and Observations (5 Marks)​
 ​1.​ ​Compare your own experimental models using the metrics list below, then compare against your​
       ​teammates' models as a team.​
 ​2.​ ​Discuss observed strengths, weaknesses, and limitations.​
 ​3.​ ​Briefly comment on possible improvements or future work.​

​Task 2 — Evaluation Metrics (report all, per model) -​​Recommend to learn the interpretation of the​
 ​Metrics​
    ​●​ ​Accuracy​
    ​●​ ​Precision, Recall, F1-score (macro, micro, and weighted)​
    ​●​ ​Confusion matrix​
    ​●​ ​ROC-AUC​
    ​●​ ​PR-AUC​
    ​●​ ​Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC)​
    ​●​ ​Brier score (calibration)​
    ​●​ ​Expected calibration error​
    ​●​ ​95% bootstrap confidence intervals for accuracy, macro-F1, and MCC​
    ​●​ ​Paired McNemar test (baseline vs. each experimental model)​
    ​●​ ​Macro-F1 and error rate per data slice (robustness)​
    ​●​ ​Parameter count, training time, and examples/sec​
    ​●​ ​Peak memory usage​



​Task 3 CycleGAN Image Style Transfer​
  ​Your Role: Generative Modeling Researcher, Computer Vision Team​
   ​Your team is entering a shared Kaggle competition to build unpaired image-to-image translation —​
    ​the technique behind production style-transfer and domain-adaptation pipelines. Each member​
     ​trains their own CycleGAN; the team's leaderboard standing and analysis depend on comparing​
      ​results and diagnosing issues together.​
​Resources:​​Kaggle Class Competition signup​ ​|​ ​CycleGAN paper​
​Instructions​

​3.1 Model Implementation and Training (20 Marks)​
 ​1.​ ​Prepare two unpaired image domains from the dataset.​
 ​2.​ ​Implement a CycleGAN architecture consisting of two generators and two discriminators.​
 ​3.​ ​Train the model using adversarial loss and cycle-consistency loss.​
 ​4.​ ​Perform image translation between the two domains.​
 ​5.​ ​Analyse training behaviour, including convergence and stability.​

​3.2 Evaluation and Analysis (10 Marks)​
 ​1.​ ​Assess the visual quality of your translated images.​
 ​2.​ ​Verify correct implementation of the cycle-consistency constraints.​
 ​3.​ ​Analyse training stability and loss behaviour.​
 ​4.​ ​Discuss the quality of your analysis, observations, and any shortcomings.​
 ​5.​ ​Submit predictions to the Kaggle Class Competition under your team and record your leaderboard​
       ​rank.​
 ​6.​ ​Participate in a blinded human audit of 30 fixed samples (style, content, artifacts) with 2 raters, and​
       ​report inter-rater agreement.​

  ​Leaderboard integrity — read before submitting to Kaggle:​
   ​Your submission must be the direct output of your own trained CycleGAN — no manually edited,​
    ​hand-picked, copied, or externally-sourced images, no hardcoded or lookup-table outputs, no​
     ​training on or peeking at test-set pairings, and no pretrained/foundation image models used to​
      ​generate or touch the submitted images. Leaderboard rank earns marks only as evidence of a​
       ​working model — a high rank achieved by any means other than your own CycleGAN's inference will​
        ​be treated as an academic integrity violation and scored as zero for Task 3, regardless of reported​
         ​metrics.​


​Task 3 — Evaluation Metrics (report all, both directions)​
    ​●​ ​FID (Fréchet Inception Distance), both directions​
    ​●​ ​KID (Kernel Inception Distance), both directions​
    ​●​ ​Generative precision and recall (or density/coverage)​
    ​●​ ​Cycle-reconstruction L1 distance​
    ​●​ ​LPIPS (perceptual similarity)​
    ​●​ ​Content-preservation cosine similarity (input vs. translation)​
    ​●​ ​Generator and discriminator loss curves​
    ​●​ ​Cycle-consistency and identity loss values​
    ​●​ ​Gradient norms / NaN count (training stability)​
    ​●​ ​Human audit score and inter-rater agreement (Cohen's kappa or % agreement)​
    ​●​ ​Parameter count, training time, images/sec, and peak memory​
    ​●​ ​Kaggle public/private leaderboard score (must come from your own trained model's inference —​
         ​see integrity note above)​
